## 3. Current Topics

### 3.1 What this group is, and who stewards what

The chair set out the group's remit: open registry protocols for verifiable AI agent identity across organizational boundaries. The ARIA Protocol was presented later in the call as one input to that work, on the same terms as any other.

### 3.2 ARIA Protocol — presentation

The chair presented the ARIA Protocol as background rather than as a proposal.

The problem ARIA frames is **accountability rather than authentication**: the question is not whether a message or action is authentic but which identified entity answers for the agent that sent it or performed it. 
A person or organization can create credentials for their AI agents, with an expiration date and a declared authorization scope, verifiable online or offline; higher trust levels add verification of the person and organization behind the agent.

The approach presented combines W3C Verifiable Credentials, a post-quantum composite signature, and DNS anchoring, such that a receiving system can verify a credential locally rather than calling a central service at decision time.

Four trust levels were described, each derived from evidence on file rather than declared by the applicant:

| Level | What it establishes |
|---|---|
| L0 | Cryptographic existence of the agent. No identity of any person or organization asserted. |
| L1 | A verified natural person answers for the agent, and the agent's domain is verified. |
| L2 | The organization is confirmed against its authoritative source of record, and the person is linked to it. |
| L3 | A person with power to bind the entity backs the agent; keys are held in certified hardware. |

The chair noted that L0 is the only level currently issued, and that L1 to L3 are specified but not yet issued.

The chair also introduced TrustLayer Foundation A.C. as the nonprofit that safeguards the protocol. The nonprofit holds the specification, trademarks and public credential ledger. The Foundation's governance provides for an independent Technical Steering Committee, which will hold normative authority once seated (formation under way, target Q4 2026). In the interim, the Foundation's council stewards the specification. This W3C group informs that Committee with recommendations on the protocol and registry. The group is not being asked to ratify the specification, and its deliverables are decided by its participants.

### 3.4 Questions and points raised by participants

**Paola Di Maio** raised the following questions written in the chat and shared document record. Answers below are provided by Aaron Grego, ARIA spec author and President of the Foundation:

1. Why some participants appeared as anonymous. 
_Meeting logistics: participants who join Google Meet without a signed-in account display as anonymous. We aim to ask all participants to identify themselves during meetings; however, it is difficult to ask everyone to turn their cameras on or display their real names._

2. Whether DNS will still be used in the future agentic web.
_DNS is steady. It has been reliable for decades, the whole ecosystem is already built on it, and that is where current deployments will happen. Parallel roots exist — on-chain agent registries among them — so anchoring in DNS should not mean ending there. I propose we take this up as a short discussion item on 22 September. See also the chair's comments below._

3. How many organizations are members of the Foundation?
_TrustLayer Foundation is a council-governed Mexican nonprofit (asociación civil) and not a membership body. It exists to hold the standard, the trademarks, and the public ledger, and to hold nothing else. Participation happens in the open: through this Community Group, through the public repository, and — once the published Verification Requirements are final — through a federation program under which independent operators can qualify as accredited registries. All documentation pertaining to the Foundation is also publicly available in the Foundation's GitHub repository: https://github.com/Trustlayer-Foundation_

4. Where is the ARIA Protocol being built? Is there a paper?
_The work is based in Mexico City. The specification is public at https://aria.bar/spec, the code and documents live at https://github.com/trustlayer-foundation/aria-protocol, and a reference registry is operated under contract by a commercial sibling entity (deliberately separate from the Foundation). On the paper: there is no academic publication yet — the public artifacts are the specification, the Verification Requirements, and our submissions to NIST's agent-identity workstream. We would rather the first formal publication emerge from this group — a Community Group report is, in our view, the right venue for it._

5. Does the work have representation in standards bodies?
_We run this Community Group at the W3C, and we participate in the AI Agent Protocol and AI Knowledge Representation Community Groups there. We take part in meetings at the Decentralized Identity Foundation, in its Trusted AI Agents Working Group, and in the AI Agent Identity Foundation's Identity and Trust Working Group. We follow the work at the ITU and the IETF, and are interested in collaborating with both._

6. To what extent does ARIA verify the existence of an agent?
_ARIA can only speak for agents that carry an ARIA credential. Within that scope, verification works by evidence ladder: Level 0 proves cryptographic existence only — key possession plus a control challenge; any name is explicitly marked self-declared. Level 1 verifies the person who answers for the agent. Level 2 confirms the organization against its authoritative source of record. Level 3 proves that the person can legally bind that organization. The level is derived from the evidence on file, never declared by the applicant and any verifier can recompute it._

7. How can ARIA track changes when people or organisations change?
_Partly solved, partly open — and the open part may interest this group. Solved: credentials carry six lifecycle states (including "superseded"); every level requires re-verification at renewal, with maximum validities kept deliberately short precisely because organizations change; revocation status is made available by the registry within sixty seconds, and conforming verifiers do not accept status evidence older than sixty seconds; every agent's authorization traces to a human authorizer and is revocable when that person leaves; and issued case files are subject to independent spot-checks. Open: continuous detection of change-of-control between renewal windows. Nobody in the field has solved event-driven re-verification well. I would like to propose "change-of-control events" as an entry in our use-cases and requirements document._

8. Is TLF/ARIA working with other standards and protocols for credentialing agents?
_Yes, by construction. W3C Verifiable Credentials 2.0 is the credential format itself; the W3C Bitstring Status List is the revocation mechanism; did:web serves as a compatibility bridge. We see entity-identifier frameworks such as GLEIF's vLEI as a natural fit for the organizational evidence branch. The specification itself names no scheme — the schemes ARIA accepts are maintained in a separate, revisable acceptance list — and we coordinate with the DIF and IETF efforts named above. The protocol composes existing credentialing standards; the only new thing is the layer at which they compose._

The chair responded during the call that the protocol's scope is identity and accountability rather than a record of an agent's full operational history, and that DNS was selected as the primary anchor because it is the only globally deployed naming system in which organizations already hold legal presence, with established dispute processes and jurisdictional reality — while noting that anchoring in DNS should not mean ending there.
